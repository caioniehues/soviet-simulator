## What this is

Yugoslav self-management was a distinct socialist model in which workers' councils controlled
enterprise decisions: production targets, surplus distribution, manager appointment, wages,
and hiring. Unlike Soviet enterprises, which received commands from ministries, Yugoslav
enterprises were self-governing within a social-ownership framework. No central plan dictated
output. Enterprises competed in markets but could not be privately owned.

This page documents self-management as a future game-mode ruleset, not as a Soviet institution.

## Target design

### Workers' councils — CONFIRMED (Britannica; AEA JEP 5:4, 1991)

Yugoslavia's 1950 Basic Law gave workers' councils control over enterprise decisions. Workers
elected the council; the council appointed the director; the council decided how to allocate
the enterprise's surplus.

**What changed from the Soviet model:**

| Question | Soviet answer | Yugoslav answer |
|---|---|---|
| Who proposes quotas? | Ministry | Enterprise council |
| Who sets reserves? | State + enterprise | Enterprise only |
| Who selects management? | Party appointment | Worker vote |
| Who allocates surplus? | Plan | Council decision |
| Who controls housing/welfare? | Enterprise + union + district | Enterprise council |
| Who approves overtime/norms? | Management + profkom | Council |
| Who coordinates inter-enterprise inputs? | Allocation orders from Gosplan | Market negotiation |

### Game-mode implications — Post-1.0

When enabled as a game mode, self-management removes the Planner's quota authority over
individual enterprises. The player sets infrastructure and social policy; enterprises make
production decisions through worker councils (AI). The player's challenge shifts from "what
to produce" to "how to create the conditions under which self-managing enterprises produce
what society needs."

Historical outcomes:
- High growth until the late 1970s.
- Inflation from the 1970s — enterprises set their own prices.
- Inefficiency — worker-managed firms tend to underinvest (voting workers prefer current wages
  over future capital).
- Inter-republican inequality grew (Slovenia vs Kosovo).

The mode teaches what happens when the Planner loosens control: some things improve
(enterprise-level coordination, worker motivation), others degrade (inter-enterprise
coordination, investment discipline, inflation).

### Separation from Soviet institutions

Self-management is not a variant of Soviet planning. It is a fundamentally different
institutional structure. The game should present it as a separate ruleset, not as a Soviet
reform — though historically it influenced Yugoslav-model socialism in Hungary, Poland, and
later Soviet reform debates.

● No CONTEXT.md exists yet (six ADRs do). I'll start one as terms settle. Three
  code explorations are running; the questions downstream of them come in round

  Round 1 — the four decisions that don't wait on code.

  ❓ Q1 — R1's cut line: The charter gives R1 six items: placement snapping,
  ghost preview with cost + refusal reason, one-level undo, tooltips everywhere,
  inspect-depth panel, selection outlines, camera easing + zoom-to-cursor. The
  UX ledger (#103) parks toolbar icons in Tier 3, riding the shell rung R11. But
  R1 already builds "tooltips on every toolbar button", and the ledger's own
  wording for the icon item is "toolbar icons rather than bracket hotkey hints, 
  with the hint as a tooltip" — that item is half-inside R1 already. Do we pull
  toolbar icons into R1, or keep the split and touch the toolbar twice?

  ➡️ Pull it into R1. You cannot write the tooltip layer for a button without
  deciding what the button shows when you're not hovering it. Splitting means
  opening the toolbar file at R1, then again at R11, and living with [1/2]
  bracket hints as the visible state through six rungs of screenshots and
  acceptance captures. It's the cheapest item on the ledger and it's on screen
  in every frame of every capture from here to 1.0.

  ❓ Q2 — The snapping model (the heart of the rung): W&R's most-criticised
  feature is clunky placement, so this is the item where we either beat it or
  don't. Four candidate layers, and I want to know which we commit to:

  - (a) A global world grid — everything snaps to an N-metre lattice, buildings
  and roads alike. Tile-game legibility.
  - (b) Road-relative snapping — when the cursor is near a road, the building
  auto-orients perpendicular to the road tangent at a fixed setback, so
  frontages line up along a street. Free placement away from roads.
  - (c) Angle snapping — building rotation quantised to 15° steps, road segments
  quantised relative to the node they leave, with a modifier key for free
  angles.
  - (d) Endpoint/node snapping for roads — a new segment within r of an existing
  road node grabs that node, so the graph never gets near-miss junctions.

  ➡️ (b) + (c) + (d), and explicitly not (a). Reason to reject the global grid
  isn't taste, it's sequencing: R8 brings heightfield terrain, road draping and 
  grade gates. A lattice that assumes a flat world is exactly the assumption R8
  invalidates, and we'd be tearing out the snapping model two rungs after
  shipping it. Road-relative snapping survives terrain intact, because a road
  tangent is still a tangent when the road is draped over a hill. (b) is also
  the CS2 move that produces the legible street-frontage look we're aiming at,
  and (d) is the one that protects the road graph from junk junctions, which is
  a data-integrity win, not a UX one.

  ❓ Q3 — Is undo diegetic? This is a doctrinal question, not a UI one. Charter
  §1: "nothing teleports; every effect has a physical cause. Fiat exists only as
  an explicitly marked bootstrap." An undo that removes a building and refunds
  its cost is, strictly, a fiat rewind — the exact thing the one rule forbids.
  Three ways out:

  - (a) Non-diegetic editor affordance. Undo is a tool, outside the fiction,
  works on the last placement regardless of state. Honest, conventional, and
  quietly breaks the one rule.
  - (b) Diegetic by construction. Undo is "the order is rescinded" — it only
  works on a site where ground has not been broken: no materials delivered, no
  work done. In that window nothing physical has happened, so removing it costs
  nothing and teleports nothing. Once the first gravel arrives, undo refuses
  with a reason, and you demolish instead (paying the real cost).
  - (c) No undo; make demolition cheap and instant instead.

  ➡️ (b). It costs the same to build as (a) — the difference is one guard
  condition — and it turns a generic editor feature into something that
  expresses the game's rule rather than violating it. It also produces a
  teaching refusal: "cannot rescind — 12 t gravel already delivered" tells the
  player that construction is physical, which is the lesson the whole sim is
  built on. The risk is that the undo window feels arbitrarily short; that's
  answerable by making the ghost preview good enough that misplacements get
  caught before the click, which is the same rung's work.

  ❓ Q4 — What does "R1 done" mean? Charter §7 says every rung gets a bench gate
  where it adds cost, plus an acceptance capture judged side-by-side against
  the previous recording. R1 adds no sim cost, so there's no bench gate — and
  interaction feel is the one thing a recording cannot show. Meanwhile #79 (G1's
  unscripted 30-minute human session) is still open and now aimed at a version
  of the game that no longer exists.

  ➡️ Re-aim #79 at R1 and make it R1's acceptance bar. The rung's whole content
  is "does this feel like hands" — that is precisely what only a human session
  answers, and we already have an open ticket for exactly that ritual. R1's own
  capture becomes a scripted 15–20 s sequence (approach a road → ghost snaps to
  frontage showing cost → move onto an illegal site → ghost turns
  refusal-coloured with the reason → place → rescind → place correctly), which
  proves the mechanics; the human session judges the feel. This also resolves
  the #79 question I left open earlier rather than letting it rot.

  ❓ Q5 — What takes the freed slot? Selection outlines are done, so R1 has five
  items, not six. The ledger's Tier 3 has two more candidates sitting
  unassigned to any rung except "rides with the shell" (R11, ten rungs away):
  edge-pan and sane pitch limits at zoom extremes. Separately, bevy_egui and
  bevy-inspector-egui are both in Cargo.toml and used nowhere in src/ — dead
  weight on every compile.

  ➡️ Don't backfill the slot; bank the saving. R1 is a five-item rung now and
  that's a real win against a 65–90 session estimate. Do drop the two unused
  egui dependencies as housekeeping inside the rung — they cost compile time on
  every iteration of exactly the UI work R1 consists of, so removing them pays
  for itself during the rung itself. If you want the debug inspector later,
  adding the dep back is one line.

  ❓ Q6 — How deep is "inspect depth"? The ledger frames item 8 as "clicking a 
  building should answer 'why is this not working' in one panel" — but the panel
  already exists and is substantial (hud.rs:1220-1398, 22 rows: kind, yard
  totals, construction phase with its Bottleneck, per-resource band state with
  DEMANDING/supplying/in-band, depot truck states, staffing
  present/assigned/needed, power). The genuine gaps are narrower than the ledger
  implies: Watered and Heated are never queried for the selected building, and
  DeficitBoard/StarvingDeficit — which knows exactly which building the
  dispatcher can't feed — is only shown globally in the dispatch panel, never
  cross-referenced against your selection. So:

  - (a) Fill the gaps. Add water/heat rows and the deficit cross-reference.
  Cheap, honest, leaves the player to read nine rows and synthesise the answer.
  - (b) Gaps plus a computed verdict line. One Line::critical at the top of the
  panel that names the single binding constraint — "NOT PRODUCING: no water" —
  derived from the same Liebig gate the sim already evaluates.

  ➡️ (b). The sim already computes this and then throws it away: run_factories
  gates on powered.0 && watered_ok && f > 0.0 and run_power_plants takes
  min(staff-factor, fuel). The scarcest factor is a value the code knows every
  tick — the panel just never asks for it. Surfacing it isn't new logic, it's
  plumbing an existing decision to the screen, and it's the difference between a
  panel that contains the answer and one that gives it.

  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  This is the shape most "add an inspect panel" tasks actually have. The
  expensive-sounding part (deciding what's wrong) is already done inside the
  simulation, because production gating and diagnosis are the same computation
  viewed from two directions. The work is finding the seam where the gate is
  evaluated and returning which factor bound rather than a bare bool — a
  one-line return-type change that makes the UI feature nearly free.
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

  ❓ Q7 — What is a tooltip attached to? Nothing exists today; hover detection
  does (UiHover for toolbar chrome, Hovered for world buildings), but it drives
  only outlines and cursor-parking. The ledger says "every toolbar button, every
  HUD number, every resource". Two shapes:

  - (a) A Tooltip(String) component you attach when spawning any node, rendered
  by one system. Simple, local, and the text lives at each of the ~40 spawn
  sites.
  - (b) A registry keyed by domain concept — ResourceKind, BuildingKind,
  ToolMode each get one canonical explanation, and nodes carry a lightweight key
  instead of prose. Tooltip text becomes a single authored table.

  ➡️ (b), joining ui.rs as a fourth primitive alongside Line, Readout and Meter.
  The same resource appears in the toolbar, the inspect panel, the band rows
  and the ledger; with (a) you write "Gravel" four times and they drift apart by
  R4, when S15 takes the resource count from 3 to 16. A keyed table also means
  adding a resource forces a compile error at its missing explanation rather
  than silently shipping a blank hover — which matters precisely because R4 is a
  resource-count explosion.

  ❓ Q8 — How far does the camera pass go? Current rig is direct and unsmoothed:
  WASD pan and middle-drag both scale with zoom distance, Q/E yaw, exponential
  wheel zoom clamped 15–500 m, pitch fixed at −45° with no player control at 
  all, and no easing on any axis. Charter R1 asks for "easing + zoom-to-cursor".
  The ledger additionally wants edge-pan and "sane pitch limits at zoom
  extremes" — but there are no pitch limits to sanitise, because there's no
  pitch control to limit.

  ➡️ Easing and zoom-to-cursor only; leave pitch fixed and skip edge-pan.
  Zoom-to-cursor is the big one — it's the single interaction that makes an RTS
  camera feel authored rather than approximate, and today zoom dollies toward
  rig.focus regardless of where you're looking. A fixed −45° pitch is a
  defensible art decision, not an omission: it guarantees every building is seen
  from the angle the models and the R0.1 shadow rig were tuned for, and it's

  ❓ Q9 — What does the ghost show where "cost" was meant to go? Roubles are the
  foreign currency and the charter is explicit that the domestic economy is
  allocated, not traded — so charging roubles to place a domestic building would
  itself violate the identity. But the road tool already models the honest
  alternative: pay_gravel drains PAVED_GRAVEL_PER_METRE from building yards

  - (b) The ghost shows the material bill — the construction site's demand in

  ➡️ (b), and it's not a compromise. The site's bill is the cost, it's already

  ❓ Q11 — Where does placement validation live? The ghost must show the refusal

  preview and the commit will disagree within two rungs — a preview that says
  yes to something the sim refuses is worse than no preview.
  - (b) Extract one pure verdict function — siting::evaluate(kind, pos, world)

  opposite operations sharing one intuition.

  - (a) Keep them separate — R stays "rebuild what I cut", rescind is "withdraw
  what I ordered". Two keys, two words, honest about being two things.

● Round 4 — the last frontier. Three findings reshape what R1 costs, and one
  document turns out to describe code that doesn't exist.

  Frontage snap needs a field the game doesn't have. Building { id, kind, pos }
  (src/sim/buildings.rs:92-96) carries no rotation — nothing in the sim or the
  save format stores an orientation for a building. Orienting a building to a
  road tangent is therefore a schema change, not a placement tweak. SAVE_VERSION
  = 5.

  Undo has a landmine that isn't obvious. Two derived caches are keyed on id
  counters staying monotonic: DockIndex on (road_ids.next_node, 
  building_ids.next, node_count) (src/sim/dispatch.rs:87-90) and PathService on
  (next_node, next_segment, count, mod_sum) (src/sim/pathfinding.rs:298-313).
  Anything that rewinds a next_* counter can make a stale cache key match a live
  topology it no longer describes — silently misrouting freight, with no error.
  Separately, BuildingEdit::Demolish captures a raw runtime Entity, which goes
  stale the moment anything else despawns.

  ❓ Q13 — Building rotation and the save bump. Frontage snap requires it. R8
  already plans save v6 for terrain.

  - (a) Add rotation in R1, bumping to save v6 now; R8's terrain bump becomes
  v7.
  - (b) Ship frontage snap as position-only — buildings align along a road but
  never turn to face it.

  ➡️ (a). (b) isn't frontage snap, it's a translation with a nice name — the
  entire visual payoff is buildings facing the street, and without rotation the
  snap produces a row of identically-oriented boxes near a curve, which looks
  more broken than no snapping. The version bump is cheap here and expensive
  later: doing it at R1 means one field added while the save has 5 rungs of
  content, versus adding it at R8 alongside a terrain heightfield. Save-version
  numbers are free; the migration is what costs, and it's smallest now.

  ❓ Q14 — What can be rescinded, and keyed on what? Six edit queues exist
  (building, road, vehicle, wire, transit, zone), plus two mutations that
  deliberately bypass the choke point — recruitment +/- and band tuning, which
  the code itself calls "player intent, not sim state".

  - (a) Buildings only, keyed on BuildingId.
  - (b) Every domain — a general edit-history stack across all six queues.

  ➡️ (a), keyed on BuildingId and never on Entity. Roads already have their own
  cut-and-rebuild pair, wires mirror it, and zones/transit/vehicles are either
  trivially reversible or physical purchases that shouldn't be. A general stack
  would have to store Demolish { building: Entity } verbatim and inherit exactly
  the stale-handle bug above. The counter hazard resolves itself under this
  scope: rescind only ever removes a site that never broke ground, so no counter
  moves and no id is reused — but I want that written into ADR 0008 as a
  constraint, because the obvious "redo" feature someone adds later would
  violate it.

  ❓ Q15 — Can R1 be tested at all? 128 tests exist, and every one bypasses the
  input layer — they push directly into the *EditQueue resources and assert on
  sim state. The capture binaries do the same by explicit convention
  (bevy.md:41: "drive capture-time action from the script, not live input"). So
  nothing in the repo exercises drive_building_tool, the toolbar, or hotkeys,
  and R1 is almost entirely that layer.

  - (a) Build an input-driving test harness — press ButtonInput keys/buttons,
  set GroundCursor, run the real game/* systems, assert on what reaches the
  queue.
  - (b) Test only the pure parts — the verdict function, snap math, tooltip
  table — and leave the wiring to the acceptance capture.

  ➡️ (a), but scoped tightly to the verdict-and-queue contract. GroundCursor and
  ButtonInput are plain resources, so the harness is small — the reason to
  build it is that R1 introduces the exact bug class nothing can currently
  catch: the ghost saying yes where the commit says no. A test that sets a
  cursor, reads the ghost's verdict, clicks, and asserts the queue agrees is the
  regression test for ADR 0009's whole premise. I would not try to test tooltip
  rendering or camera easing; those are what the human session in #79 is for.

  ❓ Q16 — ADR 0005 describes code that does not exist. It states that typed
  network topology "lives in petgraph" with a packed search mirror. In fact
  petgraph and kiddo are in Cargo.toml and referenced nowhere in src/ — road
  topology is plain ECS (RoadNode.segments: Vec<Entity>), and nearest-node
  lookups are linear scans. The union-find power solver rebuilds from scratch
  every tick.

  - (a) Mark ADR 0005 proposed — it's a plan, label it as one.
  - (b) Rewrite it to describe what's actually there, keeping petgraph as the
  recorded upgrade path.

  ➡️ (b), and drop the two unused crates alongside the egui pair from Q5. An ADR
  that reads as settled fact but describes an intention is worse than no ADR —
  I nearly shipped a wrong claim into 0007 by trusting it, which is the failure

  plugin lands — which is precisely how all eighteen drifted — while a binary
  that states what it excludes stays correct as the system grows. bench_traffic
  stops saying "I want Sim, Road, Pathfinding, Traffic" and starts saying

  where it lives.

  ★ Insight ─────────────────────────────────────
  Inclusion lists rot and exclusion lists don't, because they fail in opposite
  directions as a system grows. An inclusion list silently omits what's new — no
  error, just absence, and here absence meant a runtime Res<T> panic in a
  capture binary nobody ran until video day. An exclusion list can only ever be
  too permissive, which surfaces immediately as unexpected behaviour rather than
  as a hole. Whenever you're about to write "remember to add it in both
  places", check whether the second place could name its deviation instead of
  its content.
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────

● Fair questions — let me answer them straight, then make the calls, since
  "which is better" is you asking me to decide.

  W&R vs Factorio buildings, concretely. In W&R a building is its product: you
  need asphalt, you place an asphalt plant, and that plant makes asphalt
  forever. In Factorio an assembler is a generic box — you place it, then click
  it and choose what it makes, and you can change your mind later. The recipe is
  a separate thing you slot in.

  For this game W&R is right, and not just by taste: the charter already
  committed R4 to "12 new recipe buildings" for 13 resources, which is one
  building per product. A Soviet planner places a cement works, not a generic
  box they configure. But I'll keep Recipe as its own type with a category field
  rather than melting it into the building — that costs nothing today and means
  the Factorio split is a move, not a rewrite, if it's ever wanted.

  On the database — no, and I want to be plain about why. This is 13 rows now,
  ~26 after R4, read by the simulation every tick, never written at runtime, and
  only ever queried one way ("give me the spec for this kind"). A database or
  ORM replaces a single array index with a dependency, a query layer, async
  loading and a whole new runtime failure mode, and buys nothing. I think what
  you're actually reaching for is editing numbers without recompiling — which is
  the hot-reload benefit, and it's a real want when balancing twelve buildings.
  So: const table now, with serde derives on the spec types, which makes adding
  a RON-file override later a loader rather than a reshape. If balancing turns
  painful during R4, that loader is a small follow-up.

  Keep the enum — this one isn't close. It takes a new building from ~17 edits
  to 2, both compiler-checked, which is the whole win. The newtype version loses
  exhaustive matching across the entire tree, and exhaustive matching is
  precisely what makes 9 of those 17 touch points compile errors instead of 
  silent gaps. Dropping it would convert nine loud failures into nine quiet ones
  — the exact bug class this refactor exists to kill. It's also reversible:
  once the table exists, going opaque later is mechanical.

  Team: hybrid, as you said. Sequential phases with a review gate between them,
  but parallel workers inside a phase where the work is genuinely independent —
  the sim track and presentation track don't touch the same files.
